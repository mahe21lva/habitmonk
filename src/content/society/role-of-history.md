Most contemporary analysis is ahistorical. It attempts to explain present inequalities as if they emerged from current conditions alone, disconnected from the historical processes that shaped them. We examine poverty through income levels, unemployment through skill gaps, educational inequality through access to schools, and social mobility through individual effort. These explanations may describe what we see today, but they often fail to explain how we got here.

The present did not emerge in isolation.

The caste system, colonial economics, land distribution, access to education and the concentration of economic power are not merely background context for understanding India. They are central to the explanation itself. These structures have influenced who owns resources, who has access to opportunities, whose labour is valued, who gets to participate in institutions and who inherits advantages across generations.

Consider poverty. It is tempting to approach it as a problem of inadequate income, insufficient skills or limited economic opportunities. These are real and measurable dimensions of poverty. But they tell us very little about how poverty was created, institutionalised and reproduced. Who historically owned land? Who had access to education? Who controlled economic resources? Who was excluded from certain occupations? Who had the freedom to accumulate wealth, and who was systematically denied that opportunity?

The answers to these questions matter because inequality is not simply a distribution of resources at a particular point in time. It is also the accumulation of historical advantages and disadvantages. Wealth compounds, but so does deprivation. Access to education creates opportunities for subsequent generations, just as the absence of education can constrain them. Social networks, inherited assets, institutional access and cultural capital are transmitted across generations, often making historical privilege appear like contemporary merit.

This is where purely technocratic approaches begin to concern me.

We have become increasingly comfortable treating complex social problems as optimisation exercises. Identify a measurable problem, find the right intervention, introduce technology, improve efficiency and measure the outcome. There is considerable value in this approach. Better public infrastructure, efficient welfare delivery, accessible education and technology-enabled services can improve lives. But efficiency alone cannot tell us whether we are solving the right problem.

A solution can be technically excellent and still leave the underlying structure untouched.

For instance, improving access to education is important. But access alone does not necessarily address differences in the quality of education, social discrimination, economic circumstances at home, language barriers or the unequal starting points from which children enter the classroom. Similarly, creating economic opportunities does not automatically resolve the unequal distribution of assets, networks and institutional power that determines who can take advantage of those opportunities.

The danger is that we end up optimising within the constraints of an unequal system rather than questioning the constraints themselves.

There is another dimension to this that I find particularly uncomfortable: our own tendency to reach for solutions before we have understood the problem.

As people working with technology, building products and attempting to solve problems, we are conditioned to think in terms of interventions. We see a gap and immediately start imagining what can be built to bridge it. We look for patterns, identify inefficiencies and search for ways to improve outcomes. This instinct is useful, but it can also become a blind spot. It encourages us to frame problems in ways that make them solvable using the tools we already possess.

Sometimes, the problem is not a lack of technology, information or efficiency. Sometimes, it is a question of power, history, institutional design or the distribution of resources. These are considerably harder problems to address, and they rarely yield to neat solutions.

Understanding history does not mean attributing every contemporary problem exclusively to the past. Nor does it mean assuming that historical explanations are sufficient or that present-day agency is irrelevant. Societies evolve, institutions change and individuals make choices. But these choices are made within conditions that were not equally created or inherited.

History helps us understand those conditions.

It also introduces a degree of intellectual humility. It reminds us that what appears obvious today may be the consequence of processes that unfolded over centuries. It challenges the assumption that the present arrangement of society is natural, inevitable or simply the outcome of individual choices.

Perhaps the most important shift is to move from asking only, *What is the problem, and how do we solve it?* to first asking, *How did this become a problem in the first place? Who shaped the conditions that produced it? Who benefits from the way things currently work? And what assumptions are we carrying into our proposed solutions?*

These questions do not necessarily make solutions easier. They might even make the problems appear considerably more complicated.

But understanding complexity is not the same as being paralysed by it. And acknowledging historical injustice is not the same as being pessimistic about the future.

**Understanding history first is not pessimism. It is accuracy.**
