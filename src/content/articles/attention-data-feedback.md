There is a simple idea I've been thinking about lately.

Every system that receives feedback has the ability to adjust itself and move closer to its intended outcome.

Whether it is a machine, an organisation, or a human being, the principle remains largely the same. Observe what's happening, understand the deviation from the desired outcome, and make the necessary adjustments.

Donella Meadows puts this at the heart of [*Thinking in Systems*](/readings/book/thinking-in-systems): a system's behaviour is shaped less by its parts than by its feedback loops, the way it senses where it is against where it wants to be and acts on that gap.

But for any feedback loop to work effectively, three things matter: Attention, Data and Feedback.

Let's unpack that.

## The recurring anxiety around technology

We often hear that our ability to pay attention is diminishing. In an age of endless notifications, short-form content and constant stimulation, attention has become an increasingly scarce resource.

I agree with the concern. But I want to hold on to the thought for a moment.

While reading [*How to Read a Book*](/readings/book/how-to-read-a-book), I came across something interesting. Mortimer Adler was writing in an era when radio and television were becoming part of everyday life. He described them as "artificial props": they give us the illusion of active thinking while actually encouraging mental passivity.

It got me thinking.

Much like fashion, are our anxieties about technology and its impact on human cognition also cyclical? Every generation seems to have its own technology that threatens to diminish our intellectual capabilities.

Perhaps the technology changes, but the underlying anxiety remains.

Every tool can be misused. Yet, over time, collective intelligence finds ways to adapt and extract value from new tools. In competitive environments, we tend to move towards something resembling a Nash equilibrium, where the collective behaviour of participants adjusts to the possibilities and constraints introduced by a new technology. Patents and copyrights are, of course, interesting exceptions to this natural progression.

I'm not suggesting that every technological advancement is beneficial. Rather, I think we should spend as much time understanding how to use new tools well as we spend worrying about their potential misuse.

But let's get back to the original thought.

## The missing layer: Data

Let's look at the three components again.

### 1. Attention

Our ability to direct attention towards something is fundamental to learning and improvement. Without attention, meaningful observation becomes difficult.

And attention is increasingly becoming a scarce resource.

### 2. Feedback

Any system that receives meaningful feedback has the potential to improve towards its intended goal.

Feedback allows us to understand where we are, identify deviations and make corrections.

### 3. Data

This is where things get interesting.

Attention helps us observe. Feedback helps us adjust. But what connects the two?

Data.

Without meaningful data, feedback becomes superficial. We might know that something isn't working, but we rarely understand precisely why.

This is particularly interesting when we look at education.

## Bringing this to learning in schools

Consider how we currently measure a student's learning.

A student takes an examination. A teacher evaluates the answers, and eventually a report card arrives.

The report card might tell us that a student scored 45% in Mathematics. It might even tell us that the student needs to improve.

But what does it actually tell us about the student's learning?

Did the student struggle to understand the underlying concept? Did they misunderstand the question? Were they unable to recall something they had previously learned? Did they make a calculation error? Did they know the answer but struggle to articulate it?

These are very different problems, and each requires a different intervention.

Yet, most conventional assessment systems capture only the final outcome, not the nuances of the learning process that led to it.

We measure the result, but we miss much of the journey.

And I believe that is where the opportunity lies.

If we can capture meaningful learning data, build continuous feedback loops around it, and identify the right interventions, we can move beyond simply reporting a student's performance.

We can begin to understand when a student needs encouragement, when they need a different explanation, when they need more practice, and when they are ready to move forward.

Imagine being able to nudge, encourage or correct a student at the moment they need it, rather than waiting for the next examination or report card.

That is the kind of learning system we want to build.

## Where we go from here

We are holding on to this idea and exploring how to bring it into the classroom.

One of the frameworks we are building upon is Bloom's Taxonomy, developed in the 1950s and subsequently revised in 2001.

We believe there is an opportunity to expand how we apply this framework by connecting its different levels of learning with richer data, continuous feedback and thoughtful intervention choices.

The goal isn't to collect data for the sake of collecting data, nor to introduce AI into classrooms simply because we can.

It is to understand learning better.

And perhaps, with the right combination of Attention, Data and Feedback, we can build systems that help students become better learners, not just better test-takers.

More on that later.
