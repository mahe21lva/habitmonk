About two years ago, I wrote about [Attention, Data and Feedback](/writing/attention-data-feedback). I think it is a good time to revisit that idea and take it a little further.

While working on Bloom's Taxonomy, I started questioning whether looking at learning through its six levels alone gives us a sufficiently complete picture.

Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate and Create provide a useful framework for thinking about cognitive progression. But viewing learning as a unidirectional journey through these six stages risks overlooking some important cognitive nuances.

**Learning isn't simply about climbing the Bloom's ladder. It is also about recognising what holds us back at each step and bridging those gaps.**

Consider a student who struggles to apply a mathematical concept.

The problem could be conceptual: they haven't fully understood the underlying principle.

It could be procedural: they understand the concept but don't know how to execute the steps.

It could be a transfer gap: they can solve familiar problems but struggle when the same concept appears in an unfamiliar context.

Or it could be metacognitive: they haven't developed the ability to recognise what they know, what they don't know, and when they are making a mistake.

These are fundamentally different learning gaps. Yet, they might all manifest in the same observable outcome: an incorrect answer.

We could argue that Bloom's Taxonomy implicitly accommodates some of these nuances. But I'd rather make them explicit and examine them along a separate axis.

This gives us a multidimensional way of looking at learning:

**Axis 1: Cognitive Progression**

The six levels of Bloom's Taxonomy — Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate and Create.

**Axis 2: Cognitive Gaps**

- Conceptual: Does the learner understand the underlying ideas?
- Procedural: Can the learner execute the necessary steps?
- Transfer: Can the learner apply knowledge in unfamiliar situations?
- Metacognitive: Can the learner monitor, evaluate and regulate their own thinking?

**Axis 3: Structural Difficulty**

How complex is the problem or task? What level of difficulty does it introduce in terms of abstraction, reasoning, the number of concepts involved and the cognitive effort required?

Together, these three axes give us a much richer model of learning.

We are no longer just asking how far a student has climbed the Bloom's ladder. We are also asking what is preventing them from taking the next step, and how difficult that step actually is.

This is where things get interesting.

We now have a model that allows us to capture learning more precisely, identify specific gaps and make more informed intervention choices.

As a learner moves across these three dimensions, we can build a more detailed picture of their cognitive journey. We can identify recurring patterns, understand where they are struggling and determine what kind of intervention might help them progress.

In other words, we can begin to close the feedback loop at the cognition layer.

The ambition is not to build another assessment system that measures how much a student remembers. It is to build a learning system that understands how a student thinks, where their thinking breaks down, and what can help them move forward.

**From measuring learning outcomes to understanding the cognitive process behind them.**

That's the direction we are building.
